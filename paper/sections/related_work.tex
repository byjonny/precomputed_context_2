\section{Related Work}

Chain-of-thought prompting \citep{wei2022chain}, self-consistency
\citep{wang2022self}, and tree-structured search \citep{yao2023tree} all improve
reasoning by spending more test-time computation, but the additional reasoning
inflates generated tokens. Our study sits at the intersection of two literatures
that target this cost differently and have not yet been combined: a deliberately
mechanical prompting intervention---\emph{prompt repetition}---that improves
non-reasoning models, and \emph{distilled-insight transfer}, which shows that
abstracted reasoning artifacts guide reasoning models where raw traces fail. We
first review prompt repetition, the work most directly analogous to ours, and its
concrete prompt designs (\S\ref{subsec:rw-repetition}). We then take a short detour
through activation-level evidence that offers a candidate mechanism for why
re-exposing content can help at all (\S\ref{subsec:rw-meaningless}). Next we trace
the finding that \emph{raw} reasoning traces harm reasoning models while
\emph{distilled} insights help (\S\ref{subsec:rw-distilled}), and describe the TRS
skill cards we reuse to isolate repetition from insight construction
(\S\ref{subsec:rw-skills}). We close by positioning our controlled,
copy-count-matched design against all of these (\S\ref{subsec:rw-positioning}).

\subsection{Prompt Repetition and Context Scaling}
\label{subsec:rw-repetition}

The work most directly analogous to ours is prompt repetition
\citep{leviathan2025prompt}, which studies a minimal intervention: the user query
is duplicated, turning an input of the form \texttt{<QUERY>} into
\texttt{<QUERY><QUERY>}, with no new information added.

The authors motivate the design architecturally rather than empirically. Most LLMs
are trained as \emph{causal} language models, so a token can attend only to tokens
that precede it; the position of information within a query therefore affects
prediction. They illustrate this with multiple-choice prompts, where presenting the
answer options before the question (\emph{options-first}) forces the model to encode
the options without yet having seen the question and behaves differently from
\emph{question-first} ordering---a manifestation of the broader sensitivity of
models to context position \citep{liu2023lost}. Duplicating the full query lets
every token in the first copy be attended to from the second, approximating the
bidirectional access a single-pass causal prompt lacks. As secondary motivation,
they note that RL-trained reasoning models often spontaneously restate parts of the
user request. The attention account is offered as a \emph{motivation} rather than a
verified mechanism, and the paper provides no deeper analysis of \emph{why}
re-exposure helps---a gap we revisit in \S\ref{subsec:rw-meaningless}.

A central selling point is cost: because the repeated copy lives in the prompt, it
is processed during the parallelizable prefill stage and does \emph{not} increase
generated tokens or latency in most settings, leaving output format unchanged for
drop-in deployment. Empirically, with reasoning disabled, repetition produced
statistically significant wins on $47$ of $70$ model--benchmark pairs under a
McNemar test at $p<0.1$ \citep{mcnemar1947}, with zero losses, and the effect
tracked the proposed mechanism (options-first gained more than question-first;
retrieval-style tasks improved dramatically). Two variants are studied beyond
vanilla doubling: a \emph{verbose} form with a connective phrase between copies, and
a \emph{three-copy} ($\times 3$) form, which is comparable to or stronger than
doubling and notably better on the hardest retrieval tasks. Crucially, a
\emph{padding} control that lengthens the input with filler periods to match the
repeated length yields no improvement, isolating re-exposure of the \emph{same
content} as the active ingredient rather than raw input length.

When the same models are asked to reason step by step, the benefit of repetition
largely disappears ($5$ wins, $1$ loss, $22$ ties), which the authors attribute to
reasoning traces already restating the prompt. This near-null result in the
reasoning regime is the precise gap our study targets: it is unknown whether
re-exposing \emph{semantic guidance}, rather than the raw problem, recovers a
benefit for reasoning models. Adjacent work probes related interventions---repeating
only the question yields no gains \citep{shaier2024asking}, repeating the input
improves text embeddings \citep{springer2024repetition}, and re-reading the question
improves reasoning \citep{xu2024reread}---together suggesting that \emph{what} is
repeated matters more than repetition per se.

\subsection{Why Might Repetition Help? Activation-Level Evidence}
\label{subsec:rw-meaningless}

Prompt repetition leaves the mechanism implicit, but a separate line of work on
\emph{meaningless tokens} supplies a concrete, activation-level account of how
content-light insertions can change reasoning. \citet{shi2025meaningless} report
that inserting a long sequence of meaningless tokens (e.g., repeated slashes)
between the system prompt and the question consistently improves reasoning
performance, particularly on mathematics. Their analysis shows the inserted tokens
induce an approximately affine transformation of the attention outputs of the
meaningful tokens, which propagates through the MLP and \emph{redistributes
activations}: the fraction of near-zero activations falls while large-magnitude
activations and overall variance rise. They hypothesize that reasoning-critical
tokens (digits, operators, conjunctions) carry a disproportionate share of near-zero
activations, so redistribution strengthens exactly the representations reasoning
depends on, and they show the effect coincides with greater output diversity.

Two of their controls bear directly on our question. First, repeating an
\emph{irrelevant but meaningful} token many times (e.g., the word ``he'' inserted
dozens of times) reproduces the same activation redistribution and the same
performance gain, indicating that the benefit does not require the insertion to be
semantically informative---repetition of near-identical, low-attention content is
sufficient. Second, inserting a \emph{random sentence} does \emph{not} help: its
attention weights and value vectors are diverse, producing a structured directional
shift in the attention output that RMSNorm cannot absorb, perturbing rather than
gently biasing the representation. Position and length also matter: only insertion
between the system prompt and the question helps, and over-long insertions degrade
performance once their accumulated effect alters the semantic structure.

This literature is a double-edged motivation, which is precisely why we treat our
outcome as open. On one hand, it reconciles with the padding control of
\citet{leviathan2025prompt}: uniform, low-attention repetition shifts activations
beneficially, whereas filler periods evidently do not reach the regime that
helps---so ``repetition helps'' and ``padding does not'' are not contradictory once
the mechanism is specified. On the other hand, a distilled insight is neither
content-free filler nor a near-identical repeated token: it is \emph{structured,
relevant semantic content}, closer in form to the ``random sentence'' that
\emph{hurt} than to the uniform tokens that helped. The activation-redistribution
mechanism therefore does not cleanly predict the repeated-insight case. Whether
repeating a \emph{single fixed, already-useful} insight behaves like a benign
reinforcing signal or like an over-constraining semantic perturbation is exactly the
empirical question we isolate.

\subsection{From Raw Traces to Distilled Insights}
\label{subsec:rw-distilled}

If repetition can help, a natural first attempt is to repeat raw reasoning content
from a solved exemplar. Two findings---and our own preliminary experiments---show
this is the wrong object. \citet{wang2025harm} demonstrate that few-shot
chain-of-thought \emph{demonstrations harm} reasoning models, with degradation
worsening as more exemplars are added, even when the demonstrations are high-quality
and closely matched to the target. They attribute this to two mechanisms:
\emph{semantic misleading}, where high lexical overlap leads the model to treat the
target as identical to the exemplar and copy steps verbatim, and \emph{strategy
transfer failure}, where the model cannot extract and re-apply the underlying
solution structure. Their remedy, Insight-to-Solve (I2S), converts a demonstration
into an abstract, reusable insight and decouples solving from the demonstration
content, recovering gains where raw traces failed. The lesson we adopt is that the
transferable object should be the \emph{distilled insight}, not the trace.

Work on metacognitive reuse points the same way while emphasizing efficiency.
\citet{didolkar2025metacognitive} convert recurring reasoning fragments into concise,
named ``behaviors'' (a name plus a short instruction) via the model's own reflection
over prior traces, store them in a procedural ``behavior handbook,'' and supply them
in-context at inference. Behavior-conditioned inference cuts reasoning tokens by up
to $46\%$ while matching or improving accuracy, reinforcing that a compact,
abstracted procedural hint---not a verbose demonstration---is what reduces generated
reasoning. Consistent with both papers, our own preliminary attempts to prepend and
repeat the raw thinking tokens of a similar model degraded accuracy directly, which
is what led us to repeat a \emph{distilled} insight instead. Both I2S and the
behavior framework, however, insert their distilled object \emph{once} and vary
other axes---refinement iterations, or the number of \emph{distinct} insights---
rather than the number of repeated copies of one fixed insight.

\subsection{Reusable Reasoning Skills and the TRS Dataset}
\label{subsec:rw-skills}

The specific distilled object we repeat is a TRS skill card \citep{zhao2026trs}. TRS
distills long deliberation trajectories (including trial-and-error) into compact,
structured skill cards---organized as Trigger / Do / Avoid / Check / Risk---stored
in a key--value library and retrieved at inference to steer the model toward a direct
solution path. On mathematical and coding benchmarks, injecting a single retrieved
card reduces thinking tokens while preserving or improving accuracy, with benefits
growing on harder problems; a controlled representation analysis shows the gain comes
from structured skill reuse rather than from merely prepending relevant-looking
context. For our purposes the decisive property is that each card is \emph{pre-built
and independently validated to help at a single insertion}: reusing the released
dataset lets us hold ``the insight helps'' fixed and vary only repetition, rather
than confounding the result with our own retriever, extractor, and insight quality.
A different dataset could serve the same role, but TRS is the one release that
already pairs a target question, a gold answer, and a validated skill card, so we
adopt it here. Several related methods share the intuition that reasoning can be
transferred, compressed, or retrieved---Retrieval-of-Thought retrieves useful
thoughts for new problems \citep{ahmed2026retrieval}, ThoughtMani manipulates the
reasoning process with compact interventions \citep{liu2025thoughtmani}, and CoThink
coordinates models to curb overthinking \citep{fan2025cothink}---but none asks
whether repeating one fixed insight changes reasoning efficiency.

\subsection{The Untested Intersection}
\label{subsec:rw-positioning}

Reading these literatures together exposes a precise, untested intersection. Prompt
repetition shows that re-exposing fixed content changes behavior in
\emph{non}-reasoning models but is near-null with reasoning enabled
\citep{leviathan2025prompt}. The meaningless-token mechanism shows that
\emph{content-light} repetition can help reasoning models via activation
redistribution, while \emph{diverse semantic} insertions can hurt
\citep{shi2025meaningless}. The distilled-insight literature shows that a
\emph{single} abstracted insight helps reasoning models where raw traces harm
\citep{wang2025harm, didolkar2025metacognitive, zhao2026trs}. What no one has tested
is the cell these results jointly leave open: repeating a \emph{fixed,
pre-validated, semantically meaningful} insight before a problem for a
\emph{reasoning} model. The mechanism predictions conflict for this cell, and the
single-insertion guarantee is exactly what lets us hold ``the insight helps'' fixed
and vary only the copy count---which is what the following method makes precise.
